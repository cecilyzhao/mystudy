Topic 1: Stoichiometry & Mole Ratios

▼

📖 Concept Notes

▼

Stoichiometry: a branch of Chemistry that deals with the relative quantities of reactants and products in a reaction.

Chemical Reaction Equation — a representation of a chemical reaction. In a balanced chemical reaction, the relations among quantities of reactants & products typically form a ratio of whole numbers.

Example: Ammonia Formation
N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) → 2 NH₃(g)

Exactly 1 molecule of nitrogen reacts with 3 molecules of hydrogen to produce 2 molecules of ammonia.

Example: Single Replacement
Cu(s) + 2 AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2 Ag(s)

(clear solution → blue solution)

Chemical reaction equations tell us:

  • Reactants — identifies chemicals being consumed
  • Products — identifies chemicals being created
  • Ratios — identifies amounts of each required or generated
  • Physical states of each chemical
  • Sometimes conditions under which the reaction takes place — over arrows

Chemical reaction equations do NOT tell us:

  • Physical appearance — no info about colour, shape, density, etc.
  • Solubility — no info about the extent to which a substance dissolves
  • Pressure and temperature conditions (sometimes shown over arrow but not required)
  • Progress/process of a reaction — communicates start and end points only, not how quickly or what you will see

Reaction Assumptions for Stoichiometric Calculations:

  1. All reactions are spontaneous.
  2. All reactions are fast.
  3. Reactions are stoichiometric — simple, whole number ratio of chemical amounts.
  4. Reactions are quantitative — more than 99% complete; at least one reactant is essentially completely used up.

Predicting the Products of a Reaction:

  1. Identify what class of reaction best fits the reactants.
  2. Use the characteristics of that reaction to predict the products; include states.
  3. Balance the equation!

★ Most sulfides and oxides are insoluble (exceptions: Group 1 & NH₄⁺ compounds).

Worked Examples: Predicting Products

a) Na₂S(aq) + FeSO₄(aq) → ? (two ionic compounds = double replacement)

Na₂S(aq) + FeSO₄(aq) → FeS(s) + Na₂SO₄(aq)

★ Already balanced

b) O₂(g) + CH₄(g) → ? (hydrocarbon + oxygen = combustion)

CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(g)

★ Balance C first, H second, O third

c) MgO(s) → ? (single compound = decomposition)

2 MgO(s) → 2 Mg(s) + O₂(g)

Mole Relationships in the Balanced Chemical Equation:

For N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) → 2 NH₃(g):

  • Molecular level: 1 molecule N₂ + 3 molecules H₂ → 2 molecules NH₃
  • Molar level: 1 mol N₂ + 3 mol H₂ → 2 mol NH₃

Mole Ratios: The ratios between the molar amounts of any 2 elements, ions, and/or compounds in a chemical equation. They can be written as fractions used as conversion factors.

For the above reaction:

  • Overall ratio: 1 N₂ : 3 H₂ : 2 NH₃
  • Mole ratio for hydrogen and ammonia: 3 mol H₂ : 2 mol NH₃
n(target) = n(given) × (coefficient of target ÷ coefficient of given)
Worked Example: Malachite Decomposition

Q: What amount (in mol) of CuO(s) would be produced if 0.0045 mol of malachite, Cu₂(CO₃)(OH)₂(s), decomposes completely?

Cu₂(CO₃)(OH)₂(s) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(g) + 2 CuO(s)
nCuO = 0.0045 mol malachite × (2 mol CuO / 1 mol malachite) = 0.0090 mol CuO

★ n means "moles of"

★ Treat multiplication by a whole number ratio like repeated addition/subtraction for sig digs (maintain number of decimal places).

Topic 2: Gravimetric Stoichiometry (Mass-to-Mass)

▼

📖 Concept Notes

▼

Gravi: mass  |  Metric: measurement

Gravimetric Stoichiometry: determining the mass of a required reactant/product from the mass of a given product/reactant.

Grams A → (÷ MA) → Moles A → (× mole ratio) → Moles B → (× MB) → Grams B

Calculating Masses Involved in Chemical Reactions:

  • Balanced chemical reactions describe the mole ratios of reactants and products.
  • In the lab we measure mass, volume, and concentration.
  • Use molar mass (M) and m = n × M to find mass from moles.
  • Use n = m/M to find moles from mass.
Worked Example: Mass of 3 mol NH₃
MNH₃ = 14.01 + 3(1.01) = 17.04 g/mol
m = 3 mol × 17.04 g/mol = 51.12 g

In the synthesis of Ammonia:

N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)
  • Particles: 1 molecule N₂ + 3 molecules H₂ ⇌ 2 molecules NH₃
  • Amount: 1 mol N₂ + 3 mol H₂ ⇌ 2 mol NH₃
  • Mass: 28.02 g N₂ + 6.06 g H₂ ⇌ 34.08 g NH₃
  • Total mass: 34.08 g reactants = 34.08 g products ← Law of Conservation of Mass

Steps for Solving Gravimetric Stoichiometry Problems:

  1. Write the complete balanced equation and calculate the molar mass for appropriate components.
  2. Convert mass of given substance into a molar amount using n = m/M.
  3. Determine nrequired using the mole ratio (coefficientrequired / coefficientgiven) from the balanced equation.
  4. Convert moles of required substance into mass using m = n × M.
  5. ★ Steps 2–4 can be done in a single calculation using unit analysis. Use subscripts to track chemical species.
mtarget = mgiven × (1/Mgiven) × (coefftarget/coeffgiven) × Mtarget
Worked Example: Butane Combustion

Q: In the combustion of butane, if you have 0.929 mol C₄H₁₀, how many mol CO₂?

2 C₄H₁₀(g) + 13 O₂(g) → 8 CO₂(g) + 10 H₂O(g)
nCO₂ = 0.929 mol C₄H₁₀ × (8 mol CO₂ / 2 mol C₄H₁₀) = 3.72 mol CO₂

Topic 3: Percent Yield

▼

📖 Concept Notes

▼

Calculating a percent yield is integral to many applications of stoichiometry. We compare the calculated theoretical yield to the actual yield achieved in a laboratory setting.

Applications:

  • To test experimental designs, technological skills, purity of chemicals
  • To evaluate the quantitative nature of a particular reaction
percent yield = (actual yield / theoretical yield) × 100%

Actual yield — amount of product obtained from lab data.

Theoretical yield — amount of product obtained from stoichiometry calculation.

Experimental Uncertainties: factors in lab work that can affect the actual yield:

  • All equipment have inherent limitations in accuracy and precision
  • Initial values derived from measurements
  • Purity of the grade of chemical used
  • Washing a precipitate — can lose fine particles through filter paper
  • Qualitative judgements — e.g. estimation of colour changes
  • For school labs, 5–10% total uncertainty is typical; % yield ≥ 90% is often acceptable
Worked Example: Silver Chromate Precipitation

Q: 3.00 g of silver nitrate + excess sodium chromate → 2.81 g precipitate. Calculate % yield.

2 AgNO₃(aq) + Na₂CrO₄(aq) → 2 NaNO₃(aq) + Ag₂CrO₄(s)
MAgNO₃ = 169.88 g/mol  |  MAg₂CrO₄ = 331.74 g/mol
mtheor = 3.00 g × (mol/169.88 g) × (1/2) × (331.74 g/mol) = 2.93 g
% yield = (2.81/2.93) × 100% = 95.9%

★ Use unrounded value of theoretical yield in subsequent % yield calculation.

Topic 4: Limiting & Excess Reagents

▼

📖 Concept Notes

▼
  • Reactants are typically not present in exact stoichiometric quantities.
  • One reactant "limits" how much product can be generated.
  • Limiting reagent: completely consumed in a chemical reaction.
  • Excess reagent(s): will have an amount of chemical left over.

🌭 Hot Dog Analogy: 12 sausages + 8 buns → 8 hot dogs + 4 leftover sausages

Buns = LIMITING REAGENT  |  Sausages = EXCESS REAGENT  |  8 assembled = THEORETICAL YIELD

Steps to Identify Limiting and Excess Reagents:

  1. Convert masses/concentrations/moles of both reagents to moles of the product.
  2. The limiting reagent will produce the smaller amount of product.
  3. The smallest corresponding mass is the theoretical yield.
  4. The excess reagent produces the higher moles/mass and has unreacted reagent left over.

Alternative Method:

  1. Convert both reactants to moles.
  2. For each, determine how much of the other is needed for complete reaction.
  3. The one completely used up is LIMITING.
  4. The one NOT completely used is EXCESS.
Leftover = Total amount of excess − Amount used by limiting reagent
Worked Example: Methane + Oxygen

10.0 mol methane + 10.0 mol oxygen

CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(g)

✗ Scenario A: CH₄ limiting → need 10.0 × (2/1) = 20.0 mol O₂ — impossible! (only have 10.0 mol O₂)

✓ Scenario B: O₂ limiting → need 10.0 × (1/2) = 5.0 mol CH₄ — have enough of both

∴ O₂ is limiting, CH₄ is in excess

mCO₂ = 10.0 mol O₂ × (1 mol CO₂/2 mol O₂) × (44.01 g/mol) = 220 g

Leftover CH₄:

nCH₄ used = 5.0 mol  →  nleftover = 10.0 − 5.0 = 5.0 mol
mleftover = 5.0 × 16.05 = 80 g CH₄
Worked Example: Mg + HCl

2.20 mol Mg + 3.30 mol HCl

Mg(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)

✗ Scenario A: Mg limiting → need 2.20 × 2 = 4.40 mol HCl — don't have this!

✓ Scenario B: HCl limiting → need 3.30 × (1/2) = 1.65 mol Mg — have enough

∴ HCl is limiting, Mg is excess

nMg leftover = 2.20 − 1.65 = 0.55 mol
mMg leftover = 0.55 × 24.31 = 13 g
Worked Example: Cu + AgNO₃ (Mass-Based)

10.0 g Cu + 20.0 g AgNO₃

Cu(s) + 2 AgNO₃(aq) → 2 Ag(s) + Cu(NO₃)₂(aq)
nCu = 10.0/63.55 = 0.157 mol  |  nAgNO₃ = 20.0/169.88 = 0.118 mol

✗ Scenario A: Cu limiting → need 0.157 × 2 = 0.315 mol AgNO₃ — don't have this!

✓ Scenario B: AgNO₃ limiting → need 0.118 × (1/2) = 0.0589 mol Cu — have enough

∴ AgNO₃ is limiting

Topic 5: Solution Stoichiometry (Molarity)

▼

📖 Concept Notes

▼

Solution stoichiometry: the application of stoichiometric calculation principles to substances in solution.

  • Solvent: the substance that dissolves another substance in it — usually present in the larger quantity
  • Solute: the substance that dissolves in the solvent — usually present in the smaller quantity
  • Concentration: the amount of solute present in a given quantity of solvent or solution
c = M = molarity = moles of solute / litres of solution = n / V   (units: mol/L)
n = c × V   |   c = n/V   |   V = n/c

Conversion Pathway:

vol A → (× molarity A) → mol A → (× mole ratio) → mol B → (÷ molarity B) → vol B

Typical reactions:

  • Precipitation reactions — generate an insoluble product ← CHEM 20
  • Acid-Base neutralization reactions ← CHEM 20
  • Reduction-Oxidation reactions — involve transfer of electrons ← CHEM 30

Topic 6: Acid-Base Titrations

▼

📖 Concept Notes

▼

Chemical Analysis — two categories:

  1. Qualitative analysis — identification of a specific substance
  2. Quantitative analysis — determination of the quantity present

Procedures:

  1. Colorimetry: analysis by colour (flame test, spectroscopy, indicators)
  2. Gravimetric analysis: stoichiometric calculations from measured mass
  3. Titration analysis: stoichiometric calculations from measured solution volume

Titration: The concentration of one solution is determined by reacting it with a solution of known concentration.

  • Standard Solution: precisely known concentration, called the titrant
  • Typically a strong acid or base (reacts quickly and quantitatively)

Lab Equipment:

  • Burette: graduated glass tube with tap (stopcock), delivers known volume. 0 mL at top, increases downward. Vdelivered = Vfinal − Vinitial. Clamped vertically for accurate meniscus reading.
  • Erlenmeyer flask: allows swirling for mixing; volume measured accurately (volumetric pipette).

Titration Process:

  • Titrant slowly added until endpoint observed
  • Indicator: chemical providing visual colour change evidence
  • Endpoint: point where indicator changes colour
  • Indicator chosen so endpoint ≈ equivalence point
  • Equivalence point: reaction complete — stoichiometrically equivalent amounts consumed

pH and pOH

★ H₃O⁺ is equivalent to H⁺  |  pH: acidity/alkalinity on a logarithmic scale; 7 = neutral

pH = −log[H₃O⁺]  |  [H₃O⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ  |  pOH = −log[OH⁻]  |  pH + pOH = 14.00

Extra 9: Endpoint vs Equivalence Point (Conceptual)

Explain the difference between an endpoint and equivalence point in a titration.

The equivalence point is where stoichiometrically equal amounts of acid and base have reacted — the theoretical point of complete neutralization. The endpoint is the point where the indicator changes colour, signaling the titration should stop. Ideally the endpoint is very close to the equivalence point, but they are not always identical.

Topic 7: Titration Curves & Indicators

▼

📖 Concept Notes

▼

pH titration curve: plot of pH (y-axis) vs volume of titrant (x-axis). Curves are S-shaped (not linear).

Strong Acid + Strong Base:

  • SA in flask + SB titrant: starts low pH, rises
  • SB in flask + SA titrant: starts high pH, decreases
  • Equivalence point: pH = 7
  • Near equivalence: pH changes very rapidly

Choosing an Indicator:

  • Endpoint must be within the steep rise/drop
  • Common: phenolphthalein (8.2–10.0), bromothymol blue (6.0–7.6)

Four Curve Types:

  • SA + SB: equiv at pH = 7
  • SA + weak base: equiv at pH < 7
  • Weak acid + SB: equiv at pH > 7
  • Weak + weak: indistinct equiv point

Diprotic example: Na₂CO₃ + HCl → two equivalence points (~pH 8 and ~pH 3–4)

Drawing Checklist: ☐ Starting pH ☐ Ending pH ☐ Equiv point pH ☐ V at equiv ☐ pH scale 0–14 ☐ Volume on x-axis
