Topic 1: Mole Ratios

▼
Key Concept: A balanced equation tells us the mole ratio of all reactants and products. Use this ratio to convert moles of one substance to moles of another.
n(target) = n(given) × (coefficient of target ÷ coefficient of given)
Example 1: Silver Chromate Precipitation
2AgNO3(aq) + Na2CrO4(aq) → Ag2CrO4(s) + 2NaNO3(aq)

Ratio: 2 mol AgNO3 : 1 mol Na2CrO4 : 1 mol Ag2CrO4 : 2 mol NaNO3

Q: What amount of Ag2CrO4 is produced from 0.50 mol AgNO3?

n(Ag2CrO4) = 0.50 mol AgNO3 × (1 mol Ag2CrO4 / 2 mol AgNO3) = 0.25 mol
Example 2: Urea Production
2NH3(g) + CO2(g) → NH2CONH2(s) + H2O(g)

Q: How much NH3 is needed to prepare 1.30 mol of urea?

n(NH3) = 1.30 mol urea × (2 mol NH3 / 1 mol urea) = 2.60 mol NH3
Example 3: Combustion of Methane
CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)

a) 20 mol CH4 burned → CO2?

n(CO2) = 20 mol × (1/1) = 20 mol CO2

b) Only 15 mol O2 available → how much CH4 burns?

n(CH4) = 15 mol O2 × (1 mol CH4 / 2 mol O2) = 7.5 mol CH4

c) 12 mol CO2 produced → H2O?

n(H2O) = 12 mol CO2 × (2 mol H2O / 1 mol CO2) = 24 mol H2O

🧪 Practice Problem

For: N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3

How many moles of N2 are needed to react with 7.5 mol H2?

n(N2) = 7.5 mol H2 × (1 mol N2 / 3 mol H2) = 2.5 mol N2

Topic 2: Mass-to-Mass Stoichiometry

▼
Key Concept: Convert mass → moles (÷ molar mass), apply mole ratio, then convert back to mass (× molar mass). The "stoichiometric bridge" always goes through moles.
m(target) = m(given) × (1/Mgiven) × (coefftarget/coeffgiven) × Mtarget
Example 1: Ethanoic Acid Production
CH3OH(l) + CO(g) → CH3COOH(l)

Q: Mass of CH3COOH from 6.0 × 104 g CO?

M(CO) = 28.01 g/mol  |  M(CH3COOH) = 60.06 g/mol
m = 6.0×104 g × (mol/28.01 g) × (1/1) × (60.06 g/mol) = 1.3 × 105 g
Example 2: Sodium Hydroxide Production
2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl2(g) + H2(g)

Q: Mass of NaCl to produce 400 kg NaOH?

M(NaOH) = 40.00 g/mol  |  M(NaCl) = 58.44 g/mol  |  Ratio: 2:2 = 1:1
m = 400 kg × (1000 g/kg) × (mol/40.00 g) × (1/1) × (58.44 g/mol) = 584 kg NaCl
Example 3: Aluminium from Bauxite
2Al2O3(s) → 4Al(s) + 3O2(g)

Q: Mass of Al from 125 g Al2O3?

M(Al2O3) = 101.96 g/mol  |  M(Al) = 26.98 g/mol
m = 125 g × (mol/101.96 g) × (4 mol Al/2 mol Al2O3) × (26.98 g/mol) = 66.2 g Al

🧪 Practice Problem

2Fe(s) + 3Cl2(g) → 2FeCl3(s)

How many grams of Cl2 react with 4.65 mol Fe? (MCl₂ = 70.90 g/mol)

m(Cl2) = 4.65 mol Fe × (3 mol Cl2/2 mol Fe) × (70.90 g/mol) = 495 g

🧪 Practice Problem

Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KBr(aq) → PbBr2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)

Predict mass of precipitate from 3.50 g Pb(NO3)2. (M = 331.2, 367.0 g/mol)

m(PbBr2) = 3.50 g × (mol/331.2 g) × (1/1) × (367.0 g/mol) = 3.88 g

Topic 3: Percent Yield

▼
Key Concept: The theoretical yield is calculated by stoichiometry. The actual yield is measured experimentally. Percent yield compares the two.
% Yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100%
actual yield = (% yield × theoretical yield) ÷ 100%
Example 1: Strontium Sulfate (93.9%)
Sr(NO3)2(aq) + CuSO4(aq) → SrSO4(s) + Cu(NO3)2(aq)

1.84 g Sr(NO3)2 reacted, 1.50 g SrSO4 obtained.

mtheor = 1.84 g × (mol/211.63 g) × (183.68 g/mol) = 1.597 g
% Yield = (1.50/1.597) × 100% = 93.9%
Example 2: Iron and Copper Chloride (79%)
Fe(s) + CuCl2(aq) → FeCl2(aq) + Cu(s)

10.0 g Fe reacted, 9.0 g Cu obtained.

mtheor = 10.0 g × (mol/55.85 g) × (63.55 g/mol) = 11.4 g
% Yield = (9.0/11.4) × 100% = 79%
Example 3: Finding Actual Yield from % Yield
2C4H10(g) + 13O2(g) → 8CO2(g) + 10H2O(g)

2.98 g butane, % yield = 58.9%. Find actual yield of CO2.

mtheor = 2.98 g × (mol/58.14 g) × (8/2) × (44.01 g/mol) = 9.02 g
mactual = (58.9% × 9.02 g) / 100% = 5.31 g
⚠️ Can % yield be over 100%? Technically no — it means something went wrong: impurities, wrong product, measurement error, or incomplete drying. If you calculate >100%, check your work!

🧪 Practice Problem

S8(s) + 8O2(g) → 8SO2(g). If 60 g SO2 is produced from 50 g S8, find % yield.

mtheor = 50 g × (mol/256.56 g) × 8 × (64.07 g/mol) = 99.9 g
% Yield = (60/99.9) × 100% = 60%

Topic 4: Limiting Reagent

▼
Key Concept: When two reactants are given, calculate the product from each reactant separately. The one that gives less product = LIMITING reagent. The other is in excess.
Leftover of excess = Total amount − Amount used by limiting reagent
Example 1: Mole-Based (Al + HBr)
2Al + 6HBr → 2AlBr3 + 3H2

3.22 mol Al + 4.96 mol HBr → how many mol H2?

From Al: n(H2) = 3.22 × (3/2) = 4.83 mol → EXCESS
From HBr: n(H2) = 4.96 × (3/6) = 2.48 mol → LIMITING ✓

Leftover Al:

Al used = 4.96 × (2/6) = 1.65 mol
Leftover = 3.22 − 1.65 = 1.57 mol Al
Example 2: Mass-Based (C₆H₁₀ + O₂)
2C6H10(l) + 17O2(g) → 12CO2(g) + 10H2O(g)

35 g C6H10 + 45 g O2

From C6H10: n(CO2) = (35/82.16) × (12/2) = 2.56 mol → EXCESS
From O2: n(CO2) = (45/32.00) × (12/17) = 0.993 mol → LIMITING ✓

m(CO2) = 0.993 × 44.01 = 44 g (2 sig figs)

🧪 Practice Problem

3Si + 2N2 → Si3N4. Given 21.44 mol Si and 17.62 mol N2, find moles of Si3N4 and identify limiting reagent.

From Si: n = 21.44 × (1/3) = 7.15 mol → LIMITING
From N2: n = 17.62 × (1/2) = 8.81 mol → EXCESS
Answer: 7.15 mol Si3N4, Si is limiting
N2 leftover = 17.62 − 21.44×(2/3) = 3.33 mol

Topic 5: Stoichiometry with Molarity

▼
Key Concept: Molarity (c) connects moles and volume: n = c × V. Use this to convert between mass/moles and solution volumes.
n = c × V   |   c = n/V   |   V = n/c
Example 1: Volume of HCl Needed
Ca(OH)2(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l)

How many litres of 0.100 M HCl react with 5.00 g Ca(OH)2?

M(Ca(OH)2) = 74.10 g/mol
V = 5.00 g × (mol/74.10 g) × (2 mol HCl/1 mol Ca(OH)2) × (L/0.100 mol) = 1.35 L
Example 2: Limiting Reagent with Molarity
Ca(OH)2(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l)

15.0 g Ca(OH)2 + 75.0 mL of 0.500 M HCl

From Ca(OH)2: m(CaCl2) = 15.0/(74.10) × (1/1) × 110.98 = 22.5 g → EXCESS
From HCl: m(CaCl2) = 0.075 × 0.500 × (1/2) × 110.98 = 2.08 g → LIMITING
Example 3: Antacid Neutralization
Al(OH)3(aq) + 3HCl(aq) → AlCl3(aq) + 3H2O(l)

Volume of 0.10 M HCl neutralized by 912 mg Al(OH)3?

V = 0.912 g × (mol/78.01 g) × (3/1) × (L/0.10 mol) = 0.35 L

🧪 Practice Problem

2FeCl3(aq) + 3Na2CO3(aq) → Fe2(CO3)3(s) + 6NaCl(aq)

Min volume of 0.250 M Na2CO3 to react with 75.0 mL of 0.200 M FeCl3?

V = 0.0750 L × 0.200 × (3/2) × (1/0.250) = 90.0 mL

Topic 6: Titration Calculations

▼
Key Concept: In a titration, a solution of known concentration (titrant) is added to a solution of unknown concentration (analyte) until they completely react (equivalence point).
Endpoint vs Equivalence Point:
• Equivalence point: Exact point where stoichiometrically equal amounts have reacted
• Endpoint: When the indicator changes colour (chosen to be as close to equivalence as possible)
cunknown = Vknown × cknown × (ratio) × (1/Vunknown)
Example 1: HCl + NaOH (1:1)
HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → H2O(l) + NaCl(aq)

54 mL of 0.10 M NaOH neutralizes 125 mL HCl.

[HCl] = 0.054 × 0.10 × (1/1) × (1/0.125) = 0.043 mol/L
Example 2: H₂SO₄ + KOH (1:2 ratio)
H2SO4(aq) + 2KOH(aq) → 2H2O(l) + K2SO4(aq)

50 mL of 0.5 M KOH neutralizes 125 mL H2SO4.

[H2SO4] = 0.050 × 0.5 × (1/2) × (1/0.125) = 0.1 mol/L
Example 3: Lulu Labwrecker — pH After Excess Acid

25.0 mL of 0.525 M NaOH mixed with 75.0 mL of 0.355 M HCl.

n(NaOH) = 0.0250 × 0.525 = 0.01313 mol
n(HCl) = 0.0750 × 0.355 = 0.02663 mol
HCl excess = 0.02663 − 0.01313 = 0.01350 mol
Total V = 100.0 mL = 0.1000 L
[HCl]excess = 0.01350/0.1000 = 0.135 M
pH = −log(0.135) = 0.870 → Acidic!
⚠️ Common Errors:
• Stuck stopcock: Extra titrant added → calculated concentration of analyte will be too HIGH
• Water in buret: Dilutes titrant → need more volume → calculated concentration too HIGH

🧪 Practice Problem

83 mL of 0.45 M NaOH neutralizes 235 mL of HCl. Find [HCl].

[HCl] = 0.083 × 0.45 × (1/1) × (1/0.235) = 0.16 M

Topic 7: Titration Curves

▼
Key Concept: A titration curve plots pH vs. volume of titrant. For strong acid + strong base, the equivalence point is always at pH = 7 with a steep vertical section.
Reading a Titration Curve:
• Strong acid + strong base: steep vertical section centered at pH 7
• Acid in flask + base from buret: curve goes from low pH to high pH (↗)
• Base in flask + acid from buret: curve goes from high pH to low pH (↘)
• Equivalence point: middle of the steep vertical section
Example 1: NaOH titrated with HCl

25 mL of 0.10 M NaOH titrated with 0.20 M HCl

Initial pH: pOH = −log(0.10) = 1.00 → pH = 14.00 − 1.00 = 13.00
Veq = 0.025 × 0.10 × (1/1) × (1/0.20) = 12.5 mL
pH at equivalence = 7
Curve: starts at pH 13 → drops steeply at 12.5 mL → levels off at low pH
Example 2: HNO₃ titrated with KOH
HNO3(aq) + KOH(aq) → H2O(l) + KNO3(aq)

20.0 mL of 0.030 M HNO3 titrated with 0.040 M KOH

Initial pH = −log(0.030) = 1.52
Veq = 0.0200 × 0.030 × (1/1) × (1/0.040) = 15 mL KOH
At equivalence: H2O + KNO3, pH = 7
Curve: starts at pH ~1.5 → rises steeply at 15 mL → levels off at high pH
Example 3: Mg(OH)₂ + HNO₃ (2:1 ratio!)
Mg(OH)2(aq) + 2HNO3(aq) → 2H2O + Mg(NO3)2(aq)

50 mL of 0.020 M Mg(OH)2 titrated with 0.40 M HNO3

Mg(OH)2 → Mg²⁺ + 2OH⁻  ∴ [OH⁻] = 2 × 0.020 = 0.040 M
pOH = −log(0.040) = 1.40 → Initial pH = 14.00 − 1.40 = 12.60
Veq = 0.050 × 0.020 × (2/1) × (1/0.40) = 5 mL HNO3
At equivalence: H2O + Mg(NO3)2, pH = 7

⚠️ Note the 2:1 ratio — need 2 mol HNO3 per 1 mol Mg(OH)2
